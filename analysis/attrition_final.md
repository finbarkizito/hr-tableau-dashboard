### Summary Interpretation

<table>
  <tr>
    <th style="width:50%">Department-Level Attrition Patterns</th>
    <th style="width:50%">Year-on-Year Attrition Dynamics</th>
  </tr>
  <tr>
    <td>
      Based on the attrition-rate views across departments, the company shows <strong>moderate overall attrition with clear pockets of concentration rather than a uniform retention problem</strong>. Customer Service, Sales, and Finance frequently appear among the higher-attrition functions, suggesting roles with higher workload intensity, stronger external labour-market pull, or lower switching costs for employees. Departments such as Marketing and IT tend to sit closer to the middle or lower end of the range, indicating relatively more stable teams. The variation by department is materially larger than the variation in overall company attrition, implying that <strong>retention risk is localised and operational rather than systemic</strong>.
    </td>
    <td>
      Looking across the yearly snapshots, attrition rates <strong>shift noticeably from year to year</strong>, with different departments moving between low, mid, and high attrition positions. This pattern indicates that attrition is driven more by <strong>time-specific factors</strong>—such as management changes, workload spikes, organisational change, or external market conditions—than by fixed structural issues. The absence of a single department consistently dominating attrition suggests an organisation that is adaptive but exposed to short-term pressures, highlighting the value of <strong>targeted, department-level retention actions</strong> rather than broad company-wide initiatives.
    </td>
  </tr>
