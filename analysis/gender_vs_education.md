## Compensation Analysis by Gender and Education Level

<img src="../images/gender_vs_education.png" alt="Compensation by Gender and Education Level" width="800">

This analysis compares **average salary by education level**, segmented by **gender**, to assess how compensation progresses with qualifications and whether pay alignment differs across the workforce. Female employees are represented by the light green “×”, while male employees are represented by the dark green “+”. The accompanying figures indicate the average salary at each education level.

Education levels progress from **High School → Bachelor → Master → PhD**, providing a clear framework for evaluating returns to education and gender alignment within the company’s compensation structure.

## Key Patterns Across Education Levels

At the **High School level**, male and female salaries cluster closely in the low £60k range, with minimal separation. This indicates that compensation for lower-qualification roles is relatively standardised and largely driven by role requirements rather than gender. From a governance perspective, this reflects expected behaviour in operational or entry-level positions.

At the **Bachelor level**, average salaries increase materially, and this is where the first notable divergence appears. Male salaries trend closer to the mid-£70k range, while female salaries cluster nearer the mid-£60k range. Although the gap is visible, it is not extreme and is consistent with the broad range of roles, functions, and progression paths typically associated with Bachelor-level qualifications. This means we have a legitimate watchpoint rather than immediate evidence of inequity.

At the **Master level**, salaries increase further into the £80k–£86k range for both genders, and the gap narrows relative to the Bachelor level. This suggests that higher qualifications are associated with more structured roles, clearer pay bands, and stronger alignment between responsibility and compensation, reducing variability.

At the **PhD level**, salaries peak in the low £90k range, with female averages appearing comparable to, or slightly higher than, male averages. This is a strong signal that at the highest qualification level, compensation is primarily driven by skill scarcity and role criticality rather than gender.

## Assessment of the Compensation Strategy

Overall, the company demonstrates a **qualification-sensitive and market-aligned compensation framework**, with pay increasing consistently alongside education level. Gender differences are not uniform across qualifications, and critically, they do not widen at higher education levels. The compression and potential reversal of the gap at PhD level strongly suggests there is no systemic gender bias at the senior or specialist end of the organisation.

From a long-term compensation-analysis perspective, this indicates a generally healthy structure, with one targeted area, the Bachelor level, warranting deeper role-and level-matched review rather than wholesale intervention.

## Interpretation Limits

This analysis is based on **average salaries** and does not control for job role, job level, tenure, function, or performance. As such, it should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of gender pay equity or inequity. Observed differences may reflect role mix or career progression patterns rather than pay-setting bias. A full equity assessment would require role-normalised comparisons.

## Professional Summary

The company exhibits clear returns to education, disciplined pay progression, and no indication of systemic gender imbalance at higher qualification levels. While Bachelor-level differences merit further investigation, the overall compensation structure appears structured and defensible thus requiring targeted refinement rather than fundamental correction.
