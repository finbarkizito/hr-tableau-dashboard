Viewed through a senior analytics lens, this distribution shows a **clear and progressive relationship between education level and performance outcomes**, rather than a random or noisy pattern. At the **High School level**, performance skews toward the lower end: the largest share of employees fall into *Needs Improvement (34%)* and *Satisfactory (32%)*, with only *21% rated Good* and *13% Excellent*. This is a typical profile for roles that are more operational, tightly supervised, and execution-focused, where scope for differentiated performance is narrower and development curves are steeper. From an organisational perspective, this suggests these roles are either early-career entry points or positions with limited leverage on outcomes.

At the **Bachelor level**, the distribution shifts meaningfully upward. *Needs Improvement drops sharply to 8%*, while *Good becomes the dominant category at 50%*, supported by *30% Satisfactory* and *12% Excellent*. This is a healthy and expected pattern: Bachelor-qualified roles tend to sit in the core professional layer of the organisation, where employees are competent, productive, and reliable, but only a smaller subset operate at a truly exceptional level. Importantly, the low proportion of underperformers indicates effective role fit and baseline capability at this level.

The **Master’s level** marks a clear inflection point. Here, *Excellent (35%)* and *Good (41%)* together account for over three-quarters of employees, while *Needs Improvement falls to just 5%*. This strongly suggests that advanced education is correlated with higher-impact roles, greater autonomy, and stronger problem-solving expectations. Performance differentiation becomes more pronounced, and the organisation appears to extract meaningful value from these higher-skilled employees.

At the **PhD level**, the pattern is the most striking. Nearly half of employees (*48%*) are rated *Excellent*, with a further *34% Good*, and minimal representation in lower categories. This indicates that PhD holders are concentrated in roles where expertise, judgement, and scarcity drive performance outcomes. From a long-term compensation and workforce strategy perspective, this is exactly what one would want to see: the most highly educated and costly talent delivering disproportionate performance value.

Overall, this analysis suggests the company has a **well-aligned talent deployment model**, where higher education levels are matched to roles that enable and reward higher performance. The progression is smooth and monotonic, with no obvious inefficiencies or misallocations. Crucially, this also implies that investments in education and advanced skills are paying off in performance terms, providing a strong empirical foundation for differentiated compensation, targeted development, and selective retention strategies rather than blanket workforce interventions.
